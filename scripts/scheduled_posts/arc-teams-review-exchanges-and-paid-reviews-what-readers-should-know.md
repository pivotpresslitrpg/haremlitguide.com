An advance review copy is a free or discounted book provided before or around release so a reader can consider reviewing it. The legitimate version preserves the reader's choice: no review is required, and the author does not control the opinion.

That sounds simple, but the modern review market contains several different pathways that often get collapsed into the phrase “paid reviews.” Readers deserve a more precise distinction.

## Are Free Advance Copies Allowed?

Amazon says authors may provide free or discounted book copies as long as they do not require a review or attempt to influence it.

The current [KDP customer-review guidance](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G202101910) also says that offering anything beyond the free or discounted book—including a gift card—will invalidate the review. Amazon separately lists reviews posted in exchange for compensation among common reasons a review may be removed.

This means a normal ARC team is not automatically improper. The important questions are whether the reader was free to decline, free to criticize, and free to skip the review entirely.

## How Does the FTC Rule Differ?

The FTC rule focuses on fake or false reviews, undisclosed material connections, and incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment.

The [FTC's current business guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) explains that its Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule took effect in 2024. It does not ban every incentive for an honest review, but it prohibits compensation or incentives that are expressly or implicitly conditioned on a positive or negative sentiment. Other disclosure duties can still apply.

Amazon can set a stricter platform rule than the legal floor. A method might avoid one part of the FTC rule while still violating Amazon's terms. Authors and readers therefore have to satisfy both the law and the platform they are using.

## What Did the Review-Service Market Scan Find?

Our July 2026 frozen scan found a broad economic range, from conventional ARC coordination to services advertising guaranteed marketplace reviews.

For comparison, ordinary ARC pathways were modeled at roughly $90–$365 for a campaign aiming at 30–100 reviews, depending on platform fees and coordination labor. At the other extreme, one captured package advertised $2,375 for 20–25 guaranteed Amazon reviews.

Those figures are a historical research snapshot, not a buying guide. Vendor offers and prices can change, and the existence of a service does not show that any particular author used it.

The scan matters because it establishes feasibility. If a service advertises synchronized posting, specific marketplace coverage, or a guaranteed quantity, researchers can formulate testable questions about timing and account recurrence. Feasibility is not attribution.

## Does Reviewer Overlap Prove a Review Is Fake?

No. Repeated reviewers can arise through shared genre taste, ARC platforms, book clubs, newsletter communities, and prolific reading.

In our target-enriched archive, 43 of 264 exact reviewer profiles appeared on more than one captured focal work, or 16.3%. That percentage is not a market-wide reviewer-overlap rate. The archive was designed to collect relevant focal works, so it naturally increases the chance of seeing recurring genre readers.

Overlap becomes more informative only after fair comparison. Researchers would need to examine whether recurrence is unusually dense versus matched legitimate launches, whether the exact accounts—not merely similar display names—are the same, whether reviews refer to the correct books, and whether timing is implausibly coordinated.

Even then, the result would be a lead. It does not prove misconduct without evidence connecting an actor to a deceptive transaction or controlled account.

## How Can Readers Judge Reviews More Carefully?

Readers can reduce the influence of any single signal without assuming that every enthusiastic launch is fake.

- Read a mix of positive, neutral, and critical reviews.
- Look for details that show the reviewer engaged with the actual book.
- Treat review count, star rating, and sales rank as separate signals.
- Sample the prose when possible.
- Compare recommendations across communities instead of following one chart.

Organized reviewing can be legitimate. Deceptive reviewing is real. The line is established by conditions, disclosure, truthfulness, and evidence—not by the mere fact that several readers showed up at once.

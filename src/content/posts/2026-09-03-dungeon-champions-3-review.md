[Dungeon crawl harem](/blog/what-is-dungeon-crawl-harem/) is a subgenre of harem fantasy in which a protagonist builds romantic and adventuring bonds through dungeon exploration and monster-fighting progression. It is characterized by stat-sheet advancement, ensemble party dynamics, and romance that develops in the field rather than in a drawing room.

*Dungeon Champions* has always been a series about the team, not just the lead. By the third book, Adam Lance and Leon West have built enough history with this cast that the story can do something most LitRPG harem series can't yet afford to attempt: slow down, breathe, and let the relationships do the heavy lifting for a chapter or two. The result is one of the more satisfying entries in the series — and a confident signal that these authors know exactly what kind of story they're telling.

## What Is Dungeon Champions Book 3 About?

*Dungeon Champions Book 3* picks up with Jordan and his Society of the Defiant in the city of Debosa, pursuing a personal mission to break Sadie's curse — the ratgirl trapped in a cat's body who sacrificed herself years ago to protect Nym. The party is operating at Level 8 (Jordan at Level 13), and the absence of celestial healer Britnayel, now deployed undercover in Celestia, reshapes the group's combat and emotional calculus from the opening pages.

The authors are upfront in their notes that this installment runs lighter on combat intensity compared to Book 2, repositioning it as a connective chapter — the kind that makes the brutal action of Book 4 (which they explicitly flag as coming) land harder. That's a confident authorial choice, and it pays off in the texture of the storytelling.

## How Does the Romance Work in Dungeon Champions Book 3?

The harem development in Book 3 is earned and specific, not generic. Nym finally gets the night she's been waiting for, and the authors handle it with warmth and character consistency — her delighted curiosity and eternal optimism, as Jordan puts it, are exactly the traits that make that scene feel true to who she is rather than a milestone checkbox. Zuri's confidence reads as distinct from Merielle's protectiveness, and those aren't just personality labels — they show up in small, observable moments, like Zuri's knowing wink across a crowded kitchen versus Merielle's immediate, bristling defense of Jordan when Skullie says something tactless.

The Britney thread is the most interesting romantic undercurrent in this excerpt. Her absence is genuinely felt — Jordan describes it as "a wound I didn't realize I had until I saw it." Zuri's plain-spoken read on Britney's situation (that she's developing feelings and has complicated opinions about harems) is the kind of emotionally intelligent character work that distinguishes good harem fiction from the paint-by-numbers variety. The party is left talking about a woman who isn't there, and it makes her feel more present, not less.

## Is the LitRPG System Well-Designed?

The Battle Scholar class remains the mechanical core of the series, and it's a genuinely clever design: Jordan doesn't just fight, he *generates* the means by which his companions become powerful. The Epic Tablet system, normalized experience, the Graduation mechanic at Level 10 — these aren't window dressing. They create real stakes around who's in the party, who levels up, and what it costs when someone like Britney goes dark. The authors are also transparent about the volume of stat content, and their decision to relocate character sheets to a skippable appendix is a reader-friendly move that LitRPG series sometimes resist making.

The new quest introduced at dinner — **All The Duke's Men**, triggered by learning about conscripted men who never returned home — is a good example of how the system earns its place in the story. It emerges from a real conversation, connects to the local villain (Duke Jakken, a satisfyingly loathsome tyrant), and gets filtered through Jordan's relationship with Okanthis, a sealed god of judgment currently bound inside his Tablet. The dream sequence with Okanthis — masks tracking Jordan's movement through an impossible hall, the god communicating through pulsing light rather than speech — is the most visually inventive passage in the extract, and it does real work setting up a thematic throughline about accountability.

## What Makes This Series Stand Out in Harem LitRPG?

For readers who've worked through series like *Azarinth Healer*, *Dungeon Crawler Carl*, or *He Who Fights With Monsters*, the *Dungeon Champions* appeal is its sustained investment in ensemble coherence. Jordan leads, but the party thinks. The road conversation about adjusting tactics without Britney, the organic way the Sisterhood conspiracy thread emerges from Nym's backstory, the moment where all three women simultaneously override Jordan's attempt to have a say — these are a group that has developed genuine shorthand.

Skullie, the lich familiar currently regenerating from losing his last body, provides consistent comic relief without ever feeling out of place. His insistence that there are dangerous undead nearby (and that he's *not picky about the age of the body* for a replacement) is exactly the kind of detail that makes a supporting character memorable. The introduction of Kuir — half oni, half dragon-kin, immediately testing the party's dynamics — suggests the authors are continuing to populate their world with characters who have actual presence.
